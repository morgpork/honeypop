intro

Our story.

The spent insert pops out. A fresh one pops in. The box stays. That is the product, and it is where the name comes from.

Honey Pop is a wooden matchbox with a refillable insert. Inside the insert, honey balm — NZ honey, beeswax, coconut oil, jojoba oil. Four ingredients, six flavours, one small object you keep.

the surplus

The surplus.

In a beehive, the super is the upper box. Beekeepers stack it on top of the brood chamber when the nectar flow comes in — late spring, through summer, into early autumn. The queen stays below with the eggs and the young bees. Up in the super, workers fill the frames with surplus honey and cap each cell with wax.

Come harvest, the beekeeper lifts the super off, pulls the frames, and extracts. The brood below is never disturbed. The bees keep what they need for winter; the super is the surplus. Strong colonies produce more than they’ll eat — especially in a good year — and that surplus is what we take.

Honey Pop takes only that surplus. What ends up in the matchbox is honey the colony never needed, from hives that kept everything they did. The good stuff lives in a small box on top of something bigger, and that is the part we borrowed.

why wax and honey

Why wax and honey.

Four ingredients, each doing one job. Honey is the soothing ingredient — a humectant that pulls moisture in and holds it there. Beeswax is the structure; it’s the reason the balm keeps its shape in your pocket and doesn’t slide off your lip. Coconut oil softens the blend and carries the flavour without going greasy. Jojoba oil polishes the texture at the end — the finish that makes the balm glide rather than drag.

That’s it. No thickeners padding the jar, no fragrance compounds standing in for real flavour oil, no actives stacked for a label claim. Four ingredients you can pronounce, all doing visible work.

why a matchbox

Why a matchbox.

Japanese matchbox labels from the 1950s–70s are some of the best graphic design ever printed. Tokyo presses turned them out by the million: two or three flat colours on newsprint stock, a single hand-lettered wordmark, one bold subject per lid. Constraints — tiny format, cheap paper, a handful of inks — forced every label to be legible at a glance and nice to look at up close. They were disposable objects that ended up worth keeping. Collectors have been pulling them out of drawers ever since.

The other lineage is the apothecary tin. Utilitarian packaging designed to look handsome with wear. A tin that sat on a pharmacy shelf in 1930 still reads well today because the design didn’t try too hard. It stated what was inside, once, in a shape that ages. The scuffs are part of the object.

The third lineage is the modern refill. Aesop, Le Labo, a handful of Japanese cosmetics houses — brands that sell you a good vessel once and let you top it up. They charge for the container because the container is meant to last. Honey Pop does the same thing at a lower price point. The matchbox is yours. The insert is the consumable.

Lip balm is the right category for this argument. Balm tubes are the classic throwaway object — plastic, single-use, gone in a month. Put the balm in a wooden matchbox with a slide-out insert and the object stops being disposable. The box stays on your bench, picks up a mark from a fall, a warp from a week in the bathroom. By the fifth refill it looks like yours.

nz honey

NZ honey.

We work with beekeepers across six regions. Mānuka from East Cape, Clover from Canterbury, Kāmahi from Westland, Pōhutukawa from Coromandel, Tāwari from Northland, Rewarewa from the West Coast. Each varietal pairs with a flavour — the honey character is doing real work, not sitting in the formula as a talking point.

Single-varietal honey is a function of where the hives sit and when the super goes on. Put a hive in a stand of kāmahi during the West Coast flow and the frames come back full of kāmahi honey. Put the same hive in Canterbury clover country in December and you get clover. The beekeeper’s judgement — where, when, how long — is most of the work.

We name the beekeepers as the partnerships solidify. Provenance matters when it’s a specific person in a specific place, not a region printed on a label. That takes time to build, and we’d rather say less until we can say it properly.
