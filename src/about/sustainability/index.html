Made to keep. Built to refill.

The format is the argument. One wooden matchbox, four ingredients, one refillable insert that ships in a card envelope. NZ honey from beekeepers we know by name, zero-plastic packaging from the first box to the fiftieth refill.

our approach

Our approach

Image: wooden matchbox drawer half-open, showing food-safe insert cavity

The matchbox

Sustainably-sourced NZ pine, machined in small batches. The drawer slides on its own wood — no plastic runners, no spring, nothing to fail. The cavity is finished with a food-safe lacquer so the insert sits clean.

It’s built to last decades. Pine ages with handling: the edges soften, the grain darkens, the box starts to feel like yours rather than a new object.

The insert

The refill is a small aluminium tin — fully recyclable, no mixed materials, no plastic liner. Eight dollars, posted in a card envelope with a cardboard sleeve. Nothing else in the box.

Refills arrive ready to slide in. Empty tin out, new tin in, carry on. The matchbox never moves.

The honey

We work with six NZ beekeepers — one per varietal. None of them use antibiotics. Hive sites are rotated seasonally so the colonies move with the flowering, which is how bees work anyway.

Varietal honey, single-origin, small pours. We buy what the season gave and adjust the batch to match. If the Pōhutukawa is light one year, the Pōhutukawa balm is light that year.

The supply chain

Small batches, start to finish. Wooden boxes are machined in Christchurch. Inserts are assembled by hand in Wellington. Honey ships from six regions between East Cape and Westland. Nothing leaves the country mid-process; nothing arrives by air.

It’s slower. It’s also the reason the box you get in April was made in March and the balm in it was poured the week before.
